VSD Care Guide for Families in Pakistan

This guide is written by a parent whose child's VSD was repaired at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP). It collects information for a family in Pakistan whose child has the same condition.

This is not medical advice. Only the child's own pediatric cardiologist can say what is needed and when. Use this page to prepare questions and to find options.

What a VSD is

  • A ventricular septal defect (VSD) is a hole in the wall between the two lower chambers of the heart. It is the most common heart birth defect, affecting about 3 in 1,000 babies (Seattle Children's).
  • Many small VSDs close on their own within the first two years, and small ones often cause no symptoms. Larger ones let extra blood flow to the lungs and can strain the heart (CHOP).
  • Symptoms of a larger VSD include fast or labored breathing, the chest pulling in with each breath, trouble feeding, poor weight gain, a fast heartbeat, frequent chest infections, and a heart murmur the doctor hears with a stethoscope (CHOP and Seattle Children's).

Warning signs. The pages above do not give a single urgent-care list. Ask the cardiologist for the family's own list of signs that mean "come in today". Breathing that is suddenly faster or harder, blue or gray lips, refusing to feed, or fainting are reasons to seek care right away without waiting for an appointment.

Treatment options

  • Watching. The cardiologist checks small VSDs over time to see whether they close.
  • Medicine and nutrition. Some children get medicine and a higher-calorie diet until the hole is closed (CHOP, Seattle Children's).
  • Surgery. CHOP says open-heart surgery typically happens in the first 6 months of life when needed, closing the hole with a patch or stitches.
  • Catheter closure. Used in select cases, in older children and for specific types of VSD (CHOP).

Start with care in Pakistan

For many VSDs, good treatment is available in Pakistan at far lower cost than a trip abroad. Travel to the US is usually self-paid, needs a large deposit, and is mostly worth considering when a Pakistani cardiologist says the anatomy is complex. Ask the child's cardiologist whether surgery is needed at all, and where in Pakistan it is done well. These options should come first.

Charities that arrange treatment abroad

  • Chain of Hope (UK) flies children to partner hospitals for heart surgery. Its page does not say how families apply or whether Pakistan is covered, so ask them directly.
  • Children's HeartLink works with hospitals in underserved regions. It does not appear to help individual families.

Asking about treatment at CHOP

CHOP has a Global Patient Services team for families from other countries. Its process has five steps: first contact, sending medical records, a physician review, a treatment plan, and financial arrangements.

  • Records needed: a completed intake form, specialist reports, history and physical, lab results, scans, and a physician summary letter that covers all diagnoses, treatments and tests. Everything must be in English.
  • Costs: staff help families understand expected charges and arrange payment in advance of the consultation, through international insurance or self-payment. Do not assume the record review is free. Ask about cost before committing.
  • Phone: 001-267-426-6298, Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 5:30 p.m. Eastern Time. Address: 3401 Civic Center Blvd, Philadelphia, PA 19104.

Ronald McDonald House in Philadelphia

The Ronald McDonald House gives families a place to stay near the hospital. The details below come from the House's own web pages as summarized in search results, and those pages could not be opened directly. Check them before relying on them: room requests and rmhcphilly.org.

  • A family cannot simply apply on its own. The child must be in active treatment at a Philadelphia hospital, and an international family must be referred by a hospital social worker or hospital staff member, or by a global organization. In practice, this means becoming a CHOP patient first, then asking the social worker about the House.
  • The child must be 21 or younger, and the family must live more than 25 miles away.
  • Bring the passport and visa to check in.
  • The nightly cost is nominal and based on ability to pay, and the House says no family is turned away for inability to pay. Confirm the amount with the House.
  • Other Ronald McDonald Houses exist near other children's hospitals. See ronaldmcdonaldhouse.org. Each House has its own rules.

US visa for medical treatment

The official US Embassy and State Department pages could not be read while researching this guide. Treat this section as general background and check travel.state.gov and the US Embassy in Pakistan for current rules, fees and appointment steps. US visa policy changes often.
  • Travel for treatment generally uses a B-2 visitor visa, applied for with form DS-160 and an interview.
  • Secondary sources say officers expect a letter from the US hospital with the treatment plan, length of stay and cost estimate, documents from the local doctor, proof of funds, and ties to Pakistan (Nolo overview).
  • Reports say visa officers have been told to look closely at an applicant's health condition and ability to pay. Expect questions about how the family will fund care.

Questions for the cardiologist

  • What type of VSD is it, where is it, and how large?
  • How likely is it to close on its own, and by what age?
  • Is there heart enlargement or high pressure in the lungs?
  • Which signs mean we should come in urgently?
  • How often are check-ups and echocardiograms needed?
  • What medicines are needed and for how long? Does feeding or growth need extra support?
  • If the hole needs closing, when, and by surgery or catheter?
  • Which hospitals in Pakistan do this routinely, and what are their results?
  • Is dental care or antibiotic prevention needed? Any limits on activity?
  • Is treatment abroad needed, or can it be done well here?

Records checklist

  • Keep copies of every echocardiogram report, plus the actual echo images on disc or file, ECGs, chest X-rays, and weight and growth records.
  • Ask the local cardiologist for a summary letter in English.
  • Apply for financial help early, since many programs have waiting lists.
